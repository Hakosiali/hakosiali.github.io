AI glossary

What does "hallucination," "token," or "agentic" actually mean? Plain-English definitions, no jargon explaining jargon.

LLM (Large Language Model)

Basics

The technology behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini — a model trained on huge amounts of text that predicts what words should come next, which turns out to be enough to hold conversations, write code and answer questions. "LLM" and "AI chatbot" are used almost interchangeably today.

Hallucination

Basics

When an AI states something false with total confidence — an invented citation, a wrong date, a fabricated fact — because it's generating plausible-sounding text, not looking facts up in a database. The single most important thing to know before trusting any AI output for real work.

Token

Basics

The small chunk of text an AI actually processes — roughly ¾ of a word on average. Free tiers and pricing are often measured in tokens, and a "context window" (see below) is measured in them too.

Context window

Basics

How much text an AI can "hold in mind" at once — your whole conversation, plus any uploaded document. A larger context window is why some assistants (like Claude) handle very long documents better than others without losing track of earlier parts.

Training data

Basics

The enormous collection of text (and sometimes images, audio, code) a model learned from before it was released. It's why models have opinions, style and knowledge — and also why they can repeat biases or errors present in that data.

Training cutoff

Basics

The date after which a model has no built-in knowledge — it simply wasn't trained on anything published after that point. This is exactly why "web search" or "Deep Research" modes exist: to fill the gap between the cutoff and today.

Parameters

Basics

Roughly, the number of adjustable "knobs" a model has learned to set during training — often used as a rough (imperfect) proxy for a model's raw capability. More parameters usually means more capable, but also slower and more expensive to run.

Multimodal

Basics

A model that handles more than just text — images, audio, sometimes video, as both input and output. This is why you can upload a photo to ChatGPT or Gemini and ask a question about it.

Open-source / open-weight model

Basics

A model whose underlying weights are published for anyone to download and run on their own hardware, rather than only accessible through one company's app. DeepSeek is the best-known example covered on this site — see our full DeepSeek guide.

Fine-tuning

Basics

Further training an existing model on a narrower, specific dataset to specialize it — for example, tuning a general model to be especially good at legal writing or customer support responses.

Temperature

Basics

A setting (mostly relevant to developers using an API) that controls how "creative" versus predictable a model's output is. Low temperature gives safer, more repetitive answers; high temperature gives more varied, occasionally stranger ones.

Prompt engineering

Prompting

The skill of writing instructions that reliably get good results from an AI — role, task, context and format is the core formula. See our full prompt-writing guide.

System prompt

Prompting

Standing instructions set once that shape every response in a conversation — "always answer in bullet points," "you are a patient tutor." Custom GPTs, Gems and Projects are all built around a system prompt you set once.

Few-shot prompting

Prompting

Showing the AI one or two examples of exactly the output style you want, rather than only describing it in words. Consistently more effective than description alone — "here's an email I like the tone of, write a new one in the same style."

Chain-of-thought

Prompting

Asking a model to "think step by step" before giving a final answer — this measurably improves accuracy on math, logic and planning tasks, and lets you see exactly where its reasoning went wrong if the answer is off.

Prompt injection

Prompting

A security risk where hidden text (in a webpage, document or email) tries to hijack an AI agent's instructions — for example, a webpage secretly telling an AI browsing it to "ignore previous instructions and do X." Relevant mainly to AI agents that can browse or take actions, not plain chat.

Agentic AI

Features

An AI that doesn't just answer — it takes multi-step actions on your behalf: reading files, running commands, editing code, browsing the web. Claude Code is the clearest example on this site; see our Claude Code guide and agentic vs autocomplete coding tools.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

Features

A technique where a model answers by first retrieving relevant information from a specific source (your documents, the live web) rather than relying only on its training data — this is why NotebookLM invents far fewer facts: it's answering from what you actually gave it.

Custom GPT / Gem / Project

Features

Each major assistant's name for a reusable, pre-configured mini-assistant: ChatGPT calls them Custom GPTs, Gemini calls them Gems, Claude calls them Projects. All are the same underlying idea — a system prompt plus optional files, saved so you don't retype the setup every time.

API

Features

The way developers connect their own apps directly to an AI model, rather than using the company's chat website. If a tool on this site "uses GPT" or "is powered by Claude" under the hood, it's almost always via an API.

Rate limit

Features

The cap on how much you can use a tool for free in a given period — Claude's tight daily message cap versus Gemini's more generous one is a rate-limit difference we cover throughout our comparison articles.

Deep Research

Features

A mode (in ChatGPT, Gemini and similar) that browses many sources and compiles a structured report on a topic, rather than answering from memory alone — a genuinely useful starting point for research, provided you still verify sources.

Voice mode

Features

Real-time spoken conversation with an AI assistant, as opposed to typing — ChatGPT's is generally considered the most natural in back-and-forth conversation among the major assistants.

Free tier

Features

The no-cost version of a tool, usually with a usage cap (a rate limit) or feature restrictions compared to a paid plan. Nearly every tool reviewed on this site has one worth trying before you consider paying anything.

ATS (Applicant Tracking System)

Features

The software many employers use to automatically scan resumes before a human sees them — why "ATS-friendly formatting" matters so much in resume tools. See our AI resume builder roundup.

Spaced repetition

Features

A study technique where you review a flashcard right before you're likely to forget it, rather than on a fixed schedule — the core idea behind Anki and most flashcard apps' scheduling algorithms. See our flashcard app roundup.

AI detector

Trust & Safety

A tool (GPTZero, Turnitin, Originality.ai) that estimates the probability a text was AI-written, based on statistical patterns — a useful first signal, not proof. See our full guide to AI detectors and their real limits.

Perplexity (the metric)

Trust & Safety

Not to be confused with the AI search engine of the same name — this is a statistical measure of how "predictable" a piece of text is to a model. AI detectors use low perplexity (very predictable wording) as one signal that text may be AI-generated.

Burstiness

Trust & Safety

How much sentence length and rhythm varies across a text. Human writing tends to be "burstier" (more variation); AI text tends to be more uniform — another signal AI detectors rely on, and another reason detector scores aren't proof on their own.

False positive (AI detection)

Trust & Safety

When a detector flags genuinely human-written text as AI-generated — documented to happen disproportionately to non-native English writers, since simpler sentence structure can look statistically similar to AI output. Covered in depth in our AI detectors guide.
